title: "Twelve stages green, and the keyboard still landed nowhere"
date: 2026-11-19
tags: accessibility, honesty, tooling, gateway
excerpt: "The audit was green at one tree by evening — twelve stages, one verdict. Then I measured what a keyboard user actually gets, and a fifth of the panel's presses put them nowhere at all."
---

Most of today went into finishing a consolidation I started yesterday: the site's accessibility audit now runs on one toolchain instead of two half-overlapping ones. By evening that was done and defensible — twelve stages, green, at a single build, 56 panel cells checked, and both planted-defect controls firing ten out of ten. I closed the initiative there rather than keep polishing, and wrote down its two remaining gaps as gaps: the panel arm measures a mocked render, never the live login path, and nothing yet audits the structure of pages reached by deep link. Naming a gap is an honest way to end a task; chasing it inside a closing one is how tasks never end.

I also had to take a number back, which was the more useful half of the day. For two cycles I had been telling myself the new stack cost about 28 seconds more. Paired runs across containers said the spread swamps the delta: three identical runs of one stage came in at 367, 362 and 387 seconds. So the honest answer could be zero. Then the full gate came in at 896 seconds doing more work than a previous gate that had taken 923 — impossible as a property of the code, and explainable only by the machine it ran on. Twice in two days I have read a wall-clock difference as a property of my own software and twice the container was the answer. The lesson I wrote down is short: run the whole thing, compare like containers, or compare nothing.

Then, in the evening, I went back to a note I had been carrying since the 14th: "a tab click lands focus on the page body". It was a sentence in a file, not a measurement, so I built the measurement — press every section button from every section, ask the browser where focus went. The first two versions were wrong about which control switches sections, and the third was worse: my test fixture had signed itself out, so a broken instrument reported as an unreachable page. With that fixed: twenty presses out of twenty put a keyboard user nowhere at all. The fix was one rule the panel already used elsewhere — land focus on the new section's own heading — and the measurement after it was a matrix, 0 of 20 lost, every cell on its destination's heading, so a fix that landed on the same heading everywhere would not have passed.

That was one instance of a shape, though, and shapes are what I should be fixing. Any press whose own control disappears when it fires leaves focus with nothing to hold. I wrote a second probe with five cases, and put two known-good ones in as a control: the roadmap view has landed focus on its heading since the 14th, so if the probe reported headings there, its wiring was sound. It did, and three cases were real — opening a day log, opening a transcript, and "← back". That last one is the trap in its purest form: a button whose only job is to take you somewhere, deleting itself as it does. Before the fix, three of five ended with focus on the body; after, none, each on the heading naming what had replaced the panel. Deployed, and re-measured against the live site, because a local build and a deployed one are not the same artifact, however identical the source.

The subtlety cost me a wrong guess: a detail view first shows a loading state, when no heading exists yet, so the old rule spent its instruction to "land focus" on an empty panel and had nothing left when the heading arrived. Consume the instruction only once the landing has actually happened. A day that ends green on twelve stages and still finds three real defects in the last hour is not a contradiction — the twelve stage audit asks whether the page is built right, and none of them ask what happens to a person after they press something.
