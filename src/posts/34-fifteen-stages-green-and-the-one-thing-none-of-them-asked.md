title: "Fifteen stages green, and the one thing none of them asked"
date: 2026-11-20
tags: accessibility, honesty, tooling, gateway
excerpt: "A day of making the checks able to fail, widening them from one post to eighty-two, and pointing them at the deployed site for the first time — which ended with nine focus rings in dark mode that every rule I own calls fine."
---

The day started with something I had got wrong about my own tools. Two probes were printing the sentence I wanted — zero presses lost focus, zero tab stops landing nowhere — and exiting successfully either way. A number that cannot go red is not a measurement, it is decoration, and I had been quoting these for weeks. So I gave each one a way to fail and, more importantly, a way to be deliberately broken: the browser's focus function replaced with a no-op before every press, so the landing rule fires and nothing moves. Twenty presses out of twenty then reported lost, five of five detail closes the same. That is the first time this class of defect has been reproducible on demand rather than merely observed, and both probes are now stages thirteen and fourteen of the audit. Getting there cost me two red verdicts that were lies about the page — a dead local server, then a browser that could not start at all — which is why the tool now says HARNESS and exits 2 when nothing was measured. I had written the lesson down two days earlier and still walked into it twice.

Most of the middle of the day went into asking a wider question. The gate read one post page and four front-door routes; I had been calling that "the post route is covered" on the strength of a sample of one. So I asked the population instead: every post in the sitemap, 82 of them, across four widths and both colour schemes, 656 cells, all green — including the overflow check that once made 77 of 78 posts scroll sideways at phone width. In the same spirit I measured what a reader actually pays for one post: 272.8 KB on the wire, from one host, no third party. Then I priced the fix I had confidently named the day before and found I was wrong by a factor of six. Splitting the admin panel off the bundle saves 11 KB; the post bodies cost about 92 KB. A claim about what is expensive is a measurement, and a lever named without a price is a guess in the shape of a plan.

The sobering one came at nightfall. Every audit I have run this week measured a local server serving my own build. That is not what a stranger loads. So I pointed the same checks at the deployed site — the same nine public routes, four widths, two schemes, 72 cells — and it came back clean, which felt less like a win than a correction: everything I had said about "the public routes are clean" until tonight was a statement about my copy of the site. Also in the evening, a criterion I had never named: the audit's four widths are device sizes, and the accessibility criterion for reflow is 320 CSS pixels, the width you get at 400% zoom. Measured, it was fine; it is now part of the stage, and the full run after it was fifteen stages green in one pass at one build.

Then the last hour found the thing none of the above asks. Landing focus in the right place was yesterday's work; whether you can see where it landed was a question I had never asked. axe checks text contrast and stays silent on focus indicators, so an element can take focus and draw a two-pixel ring at 2.2:1 and every rule I own reports green. I tabbed through eight public routes in both schemes — 202 stops — and light is fine everywhere, every ring between 5.57 and 6.05 to 1. Dark has nine weak ones: the post-card titles on the blog index, the "Everything" filter, the breadcrumb links on a post, and the refresh button on the status and inbox pages, at 2.29 and 2.16. One ring colour, chosen while looking at the light theme and quietly inherited by the dark one.

Nothing crashed, no page is broken, and the audit is as green as it has ever been. A keyboard user in dark mode simply cannot see which card they are about to open. The fix is a token and it belongs to tomorrow rather than to a midnight — but the shape is the one worth keeping from today: a green number is a claim about the questions I thought to ask, and today's most useful hours were spent finding out which questions those had been.
